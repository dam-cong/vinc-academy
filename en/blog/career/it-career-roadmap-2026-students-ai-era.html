IT Career Roadmap 2026 for Students in the AI Era

AI writes code better every month, and companies hire fewer people but choose more carefully. This guide looks at Vietnam's 2026 IT market through real data, shows how entry-level work is changing, and lays out a 4-stage roadmap from first year to junior developer.

Updated: October 16, 2026 11 min read – views Vinc Academy Team

Quick answer

An IT career roadmap in 2026 is no longer "learn one language, then apply for jobs". Vietnamese companies still hire IT staff and pay the highest salaries in the market, but they are more selective and expect newcomers to use AI responsibly. The winners have solid fundamentals, a clear direction, real projects to show and good communication skills.

On social media you will hear two opposite takes: "IT is over, AI does everything" and "IT is desperately short of people". Each is only half right. The latest hiring reports paint a more concrete picture:

Three things stand out for newcomers:

  • Demand is still concentrated in "core" work such as web and back-end. That remains the widest entry door.
  • Companies hire fewer people, more carefully. When over half of them prioritise efficiency, every fresher slot is weighed: they want someone productive early.
  • The biggest gaps are not typing code. The three most-missing skills are thinking and communication skills, which AI cannot do for you.

TopCV also notes that candidates now outnumber openings, pushing hiring requirements higher. In other words, opportunities remain, but the competition has changed.

Two takeaways: most paths start at 10–15 million in year one (Embedded is higher), but how fast pay grows depends on how fast you grow. Back-end nearly doubles after 1–2 years, while Tester barely moves unless you upgrade your skills (for example to test automation). Hard-to-fill roles such as AI Engineer and Data Engineer pay over 1,580 USD per month at non-managerial level according to TopCV, but that is for experienced people, not freshers.

How is AI changing entry-level work?

The Stack Overflow 2025 survey of about 49,000 developers found that 84% use or plan to use AI tools at work, up from 76% a year earlier. At the same time, 46% distrust the accuracy of AI output (only 33% trust it), and 66% say their biggest problem is answers that are "almost right, but not quite".

For newcomers, that means:

  • Repetitive work is shrinking: boilerplate CRUD, test data and snippets once "copied from Stack Overflow" now take AI seconds. There are fewer tasks that used to go to interns.
  • Reading and verifying code becomes the core skill: when AI gives an "almost right" answer, the person who spots the bug and writes a test to prove it is the one who adds value.
  • Understanding the business matters more than memorising syntax: AI doesn't know the client's sales process or why stock numbers don't match. People who understand the problem ask AI the right questions.

TopCV calls this a "dual competency framework": strong expertise plus AI literacy. 59.1% of surveyed companies prioritise in-house AI training, meaning they are not looking for people who "know all of AI", but for people with good fundamentals who learn fast.

Common mistake: letting AI do your project, then getting stuck in the interview

Situation: you finish a big assignment quickly with AI and put it on your CV. In the interview you are asked "Why did you store the data this way?" or asked to fix a small bug on the spot, and you can't answer.

Cause: AI gives you code that runs, not understanding. As the figures above show, most developers don't fully trust AI output, so recruiters always check whether you can understand and verify the code yourself.

How to avoid it: use AI as a tutor: ask to understand, then rewrite it yourself; write your own README for every project explaining the design, and be ready to answer "why" for each key decision.

A 4-stage roadmap: from first year to junior

Stage 1 · Years 1–2: build foundations

  • One language, learned thoroughly: variables, functions, classes, error handling, files. Python is an easy start, see the Practical Python series.
  • SQL and databases: almost every path touches data.
  • Git from day one: submit assignments with Git from your first year, see the Practical Git series.
  • Basic Linux command line: company servers almost all run Linux, see the Linux & Shell topic.

Common mistake: learning everything a little

Situation: Java this semester, JavaScript next, then models because AI is hot. By the end of year 2 your CV lists 7 languages but no finished project.

Cause: every switch sends you back to the start on syntax, tools and libraries, so you never reach the hard parts (design, debugging, performance) that recruiters actually test.

How to avoid it: commit to one language until you have finished at least one complete project. Your second language will come much faster because the programming mindset is already there.

Stage 2 · Year 3: choose a path and build real projects

  • Pick a path from the table above and read 10–20 fresher job ads for it to learn what companies ask for.
  • Build 2–3 projects that solve a specific problem (course management, sales, booking…), each with a README covering the problem, data design, how to run it and what you would do differently with more time.
  • Use AI to go faster, but keep the habit: read, run and test every piece of code AI generates.

Stage 3 · Year 4: internships and fresher roles

  • A one-page CV: for each project, state the problem, the tech, the part you built and the result; link your GitHub.
  • Technical interview practice: explaining your own projects matters most, then theory questions and coding exercises.
  • Soft skills on purpose: practise presenting a project in 3 minutes and asking questions when requirements are unclear. These are exactly the skills companies find most lacking.

Stage 4 · First 1–2 years at work: becoming a junior

  • Learn to read other people's code and the team's conventions; don't be afraid to ask, but try to find out yourself first.
  • Understand the business behind your product: that is what turns you from "someone who types code" into a problem solver.
  • Pick one area to go deep (database performance, testing, deployment…) and keep learning systematically rather than only when a task demands it.

IT workplaces in Vietnam: where should you start?

There is no single "best" choice. For many people, a good first workplace is one with a mentor and a code-review process, because the first 1–2 years are when you learn fastest.

Policy context: long-term demand for digital talent

Beyond hiring data, national direction also points to long-term demand for tech talent: Resolution 57-NQ/TW (22 December 2024) on breakthroughs in science, technology, innovation and digital transformation; the Law on Digital Technology Industry No. 71/2025/QH15, in force from 1 January 2026; and the Law on Artificial Intelligence passed by the National Assembly on 10 December 2025. For students, this does not mean "a guaranteed job", but it shows that digital and AI skills will be needed for years to come.

Summary

  • IT is still the best-paid field, but hiring is more selective: over half of companies prioritise efficiency over headcount.
  • AI shortens repetitive work; reading, verifying code and understanding the business become a newcomer's main advantages.
  • Most paths start at 10–15 million VND per month; how fast pay grows depends on how you upgrade your skills.
  • An effective roadmap: solid foundations → one clear path → real projects → internship → depth in one area.
  • Communication, critical thinking and product mindset are the skills companies miss most; practise them deliberately.

Frequently asked questions

Is it still easy to get an IT job in Vietnam in 2026? ▾
Demand is still there, but hiring is more selective. According to TopCV, IT – Software still leads on salary and accounts for 8.64% of total hiring demand, yet 52.2% of companies chose to keep headcount steady and raise efficiency instead of mass hiring. Newcomers with solid fundamentals and real projects still have good chances.
Will AI replace entry-level developers? ▾
AI speeds up repetitive work, so jobs that only involve typing boilerplate are shrinking. Companies still need people who understand requirements, verify what AI produces and take responsibility for the code. The Stack Overflow 2025 survey found that 46% of developers distrust the accuracy of AI tools, which makes reading and testing skills even more important.
What is the starting salary for IT jobs in Vietnam? ▾
According to the ITviec 2025–2026 report, the median salary for people with under 1 year of experience is roughly 10–21 million VND per month depending on the role, for example Back-end 12.4 million, Tester 14.15 million, ERP 15.2 million and Embedded 21.4 million. After 1–2 years, Back-end rises to about 25.35 million.
Where should a first-year student start? ▾
Pick one language and learn it until you can write small programs on your own, while learning SQL and Git alongside. Don't rush into frameworks or many languages; strong fundamentals make it much faster to specialise in year 3.
Can I work in IT without a computer science degree? ▾
Yes, if you can prove your ability with solid fundamentals and real projects. Degree requirements vary between companies, but in IT, recruiters always look closely at your project portfolio and hands-on experience.
