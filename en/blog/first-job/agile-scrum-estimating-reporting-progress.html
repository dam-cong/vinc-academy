Agile/Scrum for Newcomers: Estimating and Reporting Progress

On day one you hear a flood of new words: sprint, backlog, daily, story points, "is it done yet?". This guide explains Scrum as the 2020 Scrum Guide actually defines it, then covers the three skills newcomers need most: speaking briefly at the Daily Scrum, estimating work realistically, and reporting progress and risks at the right time.

Updated: October 16, 2026 11 min read – views Vinc Academy Team

Quick answer

Scrum is a framework built on short sprints (one month or less), with three accountabilities, five events and three artifacts. As a newcomer you're a Developer: at the Daily Scrum, speak briefly about how you're tracking against the Sprint Goal and what's blocking you; when estimating, break work down and include testing and review; when reporting, "done" means meeting the Definition of Done, and risks must be raised early.

Scrum in 5 minutes, per the 2020 Scrum Guide

The Scrum Guide is the original document defining Scrum, written by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, with the latest edition from 2020. It's short, and many things people say about Scrum aren't actually in it. The core:

  • Three accountabilities: the Product Owner (orders the work to maximise product value), the Scrum Master (helps the team apply Scrum effectively) and the Developers (the people who build the product, including you). A Scrum Team is typically 10 people or fewer.
  • Three artifacts and their commitments: the Product Backlog with the Product Goal, the Sprint Backlog with the Sprint Goal, the Increment with the Definition of Done.
  • Five time-boxed events, as in the table below.
EventPurposeMaximum length
SprintA fixed period that contains all the other eventsOne month
Sprint PlanningSelect work for the sprint and set the Sprint Goal8 hours (one-month sprint)
Daily ScrumCheck how the team is tracking against the Sprint Goal and adjust the plan15 minutes
Sprint ReviewPresent results, gather feedback, adjust direction4 hours (one-month sprint)
Sprint RetrospectiveLook back at how the team works to improve3 hours (one-month sprint)

What do you do in each event?

EventThe newcomer's job
Sprint PlanningRead the planned tickets beforehand, ask about unclear points, contribute to estimates; don't hesitate to say "I'll need extra time to learn this part"
Daily ScrumGive a brief update on progress and blockers, and listen for dependencies on your work
During the sprintKeep ticket statuses current, send merge requests early (see the code review article), flag blockers early
Sprint ReviewYou may be asked to demo your part: prepare test data and a short script
RetrospectiveShare at least one thing the team did well and one to improve, about the process rather than individuals

What to say at the Daily Scrum

Per the 2020 Scrum Guide, the Daily Scrum is for checking how the team is tracking against the Sprint Goal and adjusting the coming day's plan. What many people don't know: the guide does not require the three-question format "what I did yesterday, what I'll do today, any blockers". Developers can choose any structure, as long as the event focuses on the Sprint Goal.

Even so, a short frame helps newcomers stay within 30–60 seconds:

VC-128 ("Needs attention" tab): absence count and 3 tests are done,
today I'm on the UI and permissions, aiming to send the MR tomorrow afternoon.
Blocker: I don't have access to staging to try it with sample data yet,
Minh, could you help me right after the meeting?

Three things make a good update: tied to the ticket and goal (not just a list of activities), an expected milestone, and blockers with someone who can help.

Common mistake: "still working on it" three days in a row

Situation: at three Daily Scrums in a row you say "still working on VC-128". On day four you mention you've been stuck on permissions since the first day.

Cause: the Daily Scrum exists so the team spots drift from the Sprint Goal early and adapts. An update with no concrete progress and no blockers takes away the team's chance to help you in time.

Prevention: always say what's done, what's next and the expected milestone; if something hasn't moved in a day, say exactly where you're stuck. If it needs deeper discussion, agree to talk right after the meeting to keep the Daily Scrum within 15 minutes.

Estimating work

The Scrum Guide says Developers are responsible for sizing work, but it doesn't prescribe any estimation technique. Atlassian's Agile guide notes that teams commonly use t-shirt sizes (S, M, L), the Fibonacci sequence (1, 2, 3, 5, 8…) or planning poker, and that each user story should be small enough to finish within one sprint. Many teams use story points to measure relative size instead of hours; others estimate directly in hours or days.

Whatever your team uses, newcomers should do one step themselves: break the work down and estimate each part. For example, ticket VC-128 from part 1:

Piece of workEstimate
Read the ticket, clarify "do excused absences count?"0.5 day
Absence count field and tests1 day
"Needs attention" tab on the course screen0.5 day
Lecturer and manager permissions plus tests0.5 day
Self-review, send the MR, address review comments0.5–1 day
Total3–3.5 days

The numbers are only an illustration. What matters is the method: each piece small enough to estimate (about a day or less), with separate lines for clarifying requirements, testing and review. When you give an estimate, add your assumptions ("if no email alerts are needed") and risks ("I've never done permissions before").

Common mistake: estimating only the typing time

Situation: you think "this is a day of coding" and say 1 day. In reality it takes another half day to clarify requirements, half a day for tests and a day for review fixes; the ticket ends up three times over the estimate.

Cause: work only counts as done when it meets the team's Definition of Done, which usually includes tests and review, not when the code runs on your machine. Estimating only the coding misses most of the effort.

Prevention: break the work down as above, always with lines for testing and review; compare with similar work you've done; and when unsure, give a range ("2–3 days") plus risks instead of an optimistic single number.

Reporting progress and risks

"Done" means meeting the Definition of Done

In the 2020 Scrum Guide, this definition is the commitment tied to the Increment: the team's shared description of the state work must reach to count as done. Ask your team what it includes (reviewed? tested? verified on staging?). When someone asks "is it done?", answer against that definition, not "the code is written".

Template for flagging a possible delay

[VC-128] Risk of a 1-day delay (Thursday → Friday)
Reason: permissions need a new user group I didn't anticipate.
Done so far: absence count + tests, UI tab.
Options: I do permissions today and send the MR Friday morning;
or, to keep Thursday, we split permissions into a separate ticket.
I need: your call on which option works best.

A good update covers five points: how much it slips, why, what's done, the options, and what you need. Raising it while there's still room to adjust is always valued more than raising it on the last day.

Contributing at the Retrospective

The Retrospective is where the team reviews how it works in order to improve. Newcomers often stay quiet because they feel inexperienced, yet a newcomer's perspective is very valuable: you see clearly which docs are missing and which onboarding steps are hard. Comment on the process ("the README is missing the sample-data step, it cost me half a day") rather than people, and propose something concrete you can do (such as adding that step to the README yourself).

Summary

  • The 2020 Scrum Guide defines three accountabilities, five time-boxed events and three artifacts tied to the Product Goal, Sprint Goal and Definition of Done.
  • The Daily Scrum doesn't require the three-question format; keep it short, tied to the ticket, with a milestone and clear blockers.
  • Scrum doesn't prescribe estimation techniques; newcomers should break work down and include clarification, testing and review, with assumptions and risks.
  • "Done" means meeting the team's Definition of Done; when a delay looms, flag it early with the slip, reason, options and what you need.
  • Comment on the process at the Retrospective; a newcomer's view is genuinely valuable.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Daily Scrum require answering 3 questions? ▾
No. The 2020 Scrum Guide lets Developers choose how to run the Daily Scrum, as long as the 15-minute event stays centred on the Sprint Goal and ends with a plan for the coming day. Yesterday, today, blockers is a common format, not a rule.
What are story points, and are they required in Scrum? ▾
Story points are a way to estimate the relative size of work. The Scrum Guide doesn't prescribe any estimation technique; it only says Developers are responsible for sizing work. Teams may use story points, t-shirt sizes (S, M, L), the Fibonacci sequence, planning poker or estimates in hours and days.
How long is a sprint? ▾
According to the 2020 Scrum Guide, a sprint has a fixed length of one month or less. Many teams choose one- or two-week sprints; the exact length is the team's choice and is kept stable to make planning easier.
How should a newcomer estimate to avoid running late? ▾
Break the work into pieces of about a day or less, estimate each and add them up, and remember to include clarifying requirements, writing tests, self-review and addressing review comments. State your assumptions and risks with the estimate, and flag early if it starts slipping.
What is the Definition of Done? ▾
It's the team's shared description of the state work must reach to count as done, for example reviewed, tested and meeting quality standards. In the 2020 Scrum Guide it is the commitment tied to the Increment; work that doesn't meet it isn't done yet.
